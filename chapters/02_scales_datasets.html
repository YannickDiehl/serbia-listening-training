2. Scales and Analysis Datasets

NoteAbout this chapter

The two datasets of all later chapters: one row per questionnaire (df) and one row per person (df_wide). Six processing steps lead there: the items (reverse-keyed items turned around, the need-satisfaction items named by their subscales), the 23 preregistered scales, their scores and the three hypothesis composites, the design variables of the models, the codebook, and the dataset with one row per person. Each processing step is shown in the same six steps; a table at the end puts them side by side. Feeds the description of the measures in the Method section and Table 1.

source("R/packages.R")                  # all packages (Chapter 1)
source("R/settings.R")                  # waves, conditions, folders (Chapter 1)
x <- readRDS("cache/data_clean.rds")    # the analysis file (Chapter 1)

2.1 How each processing step is examined

What happens at each processing step? The same six steps as in Chapter 1, in three parts and always in the same order: the problem, the rule and the result. The diagram shows the path; the list below describes each step.

The six steps in detail:

The problem:

  1. What the data look like. The dataset as the processing step finds it: the items, answers or columns it is about. The step keeps a copy of this dataset (…_before) where Step 5 compares.
  2. The problem. What the later analyses could not use, or would misread, if the data stayed as they are.

The rule:

  1. The rule. The change, written once in a code file of its own (R/ch02_….R, R/scales.R) and applied to every questionnaire alike.
  2. The evidence per case. Every item, scale or person the rule touches, with the data it rests on. An item counts as reverse-keyed if a high answer means less of its construct, by its wording. A score is the mean of its items if at least one is answered. An item that is answered almost always at the top of the scale in January (a mean above 9.7) cannot show a change and leaves the score.

The result:

  1. Before and after. The rows, persons and columns of the dataset before and after the processing step, and what changed for the cases of Step 4.
  2. What follows. Which chapters use the result, and for what.

Next → Step 2, the problem: Which items point the other way?

Step 2 · The problem all questionnaires

Which items point the other way? Every item is answered from 0 to 10. For 22 items a high answer means less of the construct, such as “I feel excluded from the group I want to belong to” for relatedness; averaged with the other items as they stand, they would cancel them out. The names of the need-satisfaction items do not say which subscale they belong to. And the wave, the condition and sex are stored as numbers or as text that the models cannot order.

Next → Step 3, the rule: What changes?

The rule · Steps 3 and 4

Step 3 · The rule all questionnaires

What changes? The 22 reverse-keyed items are turned around (10 − x), so that high always means more; the need-satisfaction items are named by their subscales, and alienation and isolation by what a high answer now means (no alienation, no isolation); the wave, the condition and sex become factors with their labels:

# Items: reverse-keyed items turned around, the need-satisfaction block named
# by its six subscales, and the design columns as factors

  ~item,                 ~others,
  "Flourishing5",        str_c("Flourishing", c(1:4, 7)),

The rule · Steps 3 and 4

Step 2 · The problem all questionnaires

How is a scale scored with blank answers? A score that required every item would lose every questionnaire with one blank answer. And a composite of a hypothesis block can weigh each scale equally, as the mean of the scale scores, or each item, as the mean of all items of the block.

The rule · Steps 3 and 4

Step 2 · The problem all persons

What do the latent and the mediation models need? One row per person, with every wave’s answers side by side. Age and sex, however, are given on every questionnaire and need not agree between a person’s waves.

Next → Step 3, the rule: How are the waves put side by side?

The rule · Steps 3 and 4

Step 3 · The rule all persons

How are the waves put side by side? The items and the composites of each wave in columns of their own, and for each person one age (the mean of the ages given) and one sex (the one given most often):

# One row per person: the items of every wave side by side (ListenOther1_T0,
# ...), age, sex and attendance, the centred covariates of the structured
# means models (Chapter 11), the composites per wave for the mediation models
# (Chapter 12), and one dummy per training round

# sex: the answer given most often (one person gave F and M once each; the
# tie goes to "Female", the first in alphabetical order)
modal_sex <- df |>
  drop_na(sex) |>
  count(id, sex) |>
  slice_max(n, n = 1, by = id, with_ties = FALSE) |>
  select(id, sex)

person <- df |>
  group_by(id) |>
  summarise(age     = mean(age, na.rm = TRUE),
            n_waves = n_distinct(Occasion),
            .groups = "drop") |>
  left_join(modal_sex, by = "id") |>
  mutate(age_c  = age - mean(age, na.rm = TRUE),   # centred at the mean
         female = case_match(sex, "Female" ~ 1, "Male" ~ 0))

df_wide <- df |>
  filter(!is.na(condition)) |>
  select(id, condition, Occasion, all_of(item_names)) |>
  pivot_wider(id_cols     = c(id, condition),
              names_from  = Occasion,
              values_from = all_of(item_names),
              names_glue  = "{.value}_{Occasion}") |>
  left_join(person, by = "id") |>
  left_join(distinct(df, id, Municipality), by = "id")

# the three composites per wave, named as the mediation models expect
mediation_composites <- df |>
  select(id, Occasion,
         Hypothesis1_ListeningTogetherness = H1_ListeningTogetherness,
         Hypothesis2_CognitiveComplexity   = H2_CognitiveComplexity,
         Hypothesis3_Wellbeing             = H3_Wellbeing) |>
  pivot_wider(id_cols     = id,
              names_from  = Occasion,
              names_glue  = "{Occasion}_{.value}",
              values_from = starts_with("Hypothesis"))

df_wide <- df_wide |>
  left_join(mediation_composites, by = "id") |>
  mutate(train_eg = ifelse(condition == "Experimental", 1, 0),  # trained T0-T1
         train_cg = ifelse(condition == "Control", 1, 0))       # trained T2-T3
