20.1 Three estimates of the training effect

Where do the methods agree? While the experimental group trains (T0 to T1), the effect of each scale in three estimates: the descriptive Δd (Chapter 9), the preregistered multilevel interaction at T1 (Chapter 10) and the latent difference-in-differences (Chapter 11, only where the scale is measured invariantly over time, Chapter 7). methods counts the estimates that are significant (p < .05) in favour of the training.

significant_up <- \(estimate, p) !is.na(p) & p < .05 & estimate > 0

agreement <- scales |>
  select(outcome = scale, label, block) |>
  left_join(means$effects |>
              filter(pre == "T0", post == "T1") |>
              select(outcome, delta_d, p_delta_d = p),
            by = "outcome") |>
  left_join(multilevel$interactions |>
              filter(wave == "T1") |>
              select(outcome, b_mlm = b, p_mlm = p),
            by = "outcome") |>
  left_join(smm$training_effects |>
              filter(scalar) |>
              select(outcome = construct, did_latent = DiD_T01_est,
                     p_latent = DiD_T01_pvalue),
            by = "outcome") |>
  mutate(methods = significant_up(delta_d, p_delta_d) +
           significant_up(b_mlm, p_mlm) +
           significant_up(did_latent, p_latent))

7 scales show the effect in all three estimates: Others Listen to Me, Positivity Resonance, Rogerian Schema, Paradox Mindset, Autonomy Satisfaction, Relatedness Satisfaction, and Competence Satisfaction. All of them belong to listening and togetherness, cognitive complexity and need satisfaction. 12 scales show it in none, among them the frustration scales of need satisfaction, Intellectual Humility and Agency. No Alienation moves against the training in all three estimates.

And the hypothesis composites? Only Cognitive Complexity (H2) shows a clear effect in both manifest estimates:

Each composite pools constructs that Chapter 8 shows to be distinct, so an effect on a composite summarises effects of different size on its parts.

Is the latent effect larger than the manifest one? The latent models correct for measurement error, which should make effects larger. Δd and the latent d (Chapter 11.7) of the scalar invariant constructs, while the experimental group trains:

sizes <- means$effects |>

  select(outcome, delta_d) |>

               filter(scalar) |>
               select(outcome = construct, latent_d = T01),
             by = "outcome")
sizes |>

  mutate(across(where(is.double), \(x) round(x, 2)))
constructs median_dd median_d r
20 0.19 0.18 0.95

They are not: the latent effects are about as large as the manifest ones (median 0.18 against 0.19), and the two rank the constructs alike (r = .95). The correction for measurement error changes the precision of the estimates more than their size.

20.2 The wait-list replication

Does the effect return when the control group trains? The mirrored estimates for T2 to T3 (negative: the control group gains more):

# scales with a significant effect of the trained group in one window
count_dd <- \(from, to, sign) means$effects |>
  filter(pre == from, post == to, outcome %in% scales$scale, p < .05,
         sign * delta_d > 0) |>
  nrow()
count_piecewise <- \(group, sign) multilevel$piecewise |>
  filter(training == group, outcome %in% scales$scale, p < .05,
         sign * b > 0) |>
  nrow()
count_latent <- \(estimate, p, sign) smm$training_effects |>
  filter(scalar, .data[[p]] < .05, sign * .data[[estimate]] > 0) |>
  nrow()

replication <- tibble(

The effect returns while the control group trains, in all three estimates: for 9 and 8 scales in the manifest estimates (against 9 and 9 in the first window), and for 4 constructs in the latent estimate (against 8). None of the 22 replication tests finds that the two training effects differ, although the wide intervals leave room for differences.

In both training windows the indirect effect on well-being through listening and togetherness and through cognitive complexity is significant, while the direct path is not: an indirect-only mediation (Zhao et al., 2010). While the experimental group trains, the total effect of the training on well-being is not significant (0.10, p = .273), so the results show an indirect effect, not an effect on well-being that runs entirely through the mediators; while the control group trains, the total effect is significant (0.21, p = .025).

With latent blocks the total indirect effect and the serial path carry in both training windows and are larger than with the composites; the split into single paths does not hold. The post hoc structure of Chapters 15 to 19 tells the same story with fewer scores: one factor of engagement (listening, resonance, paradox and Rogerian thinking, tolerance, humility) that moves with the training, and one of well-being and agency that follows it, with indirect effects about as large as those of the composites (Chapter 19.5).

20.4 Where the results are fragile

What limits these conclusions?

Seven of the 22 latent models end at a boundary (autocorrelated residuals beyond ±1 or negative residual variances), so their latent estimates are read with caution. Two constructs are not measured invariantly over time, and Autonomy Satisfaction joins them under the stricter cutoffs for small samples (Chapter 7.3). Before the training, 9 of the 22 constructs are not measured alike in the two groups, among them Rogerian Schema, Paradox Mindset, and Relatedness Satisfaction, which show the effect in all three estimates; their latent effects assume equal measurement in both groups. By Cohen’s labels the latent effects are small (Chapter 11.7).

Correcting for testing 23 scales at once (Benjamini-Hochberg) leaves 1 scale with a Δd for the training and 6 of the eight latent training effects; tested one-sided, as the hypotheses are directional, all three composites show the multilevel effect at T1. With the standard deviation of all questionnaires before the window, the replication while the control group trains shrinks from 9 to 5 scales (Chapter 9.4). Other choices leave the picture in place: a trainer level adds nothing to the multilevel models (Chapter 10.2), and dropout does not explain the late gains in well-being (Chapter 9.6). Δd and the latent models leave out that persons are clustered in municipalities; the multilevel models, which take the municipalities into account, and the cluster-robust mediation give the same picture.

The effect on Cognitive Complexity holds in eight of nine sample sets and misses significance only among the 127 persons with all four waves. At the level of single persons, no scale separates the groups significantly (Chapter 13). The post hoc structure was found in the same data and partly selected for responsiveness to the training, and neither of its two factors is scalar invariant over the waves (Chapter 19.6), so its effects rest on manifest scores and need a new sample.
